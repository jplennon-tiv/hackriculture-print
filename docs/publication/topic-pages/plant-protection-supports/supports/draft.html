Growing Techniques - Plant care

SupportsBeans and peas

Put supports in at sowing or planting time, while plants are small. Allow for the weight of the crop and the wind catching its leaves: a frame that looks sturdy when empty has a much harder job later.

The traditional bean row

For climbing beans, push two rows of stout canes firmly into the ground and lean each opposing pair together. Cross them near the top, then tie a single horizontal cane along the ridge to join the pairs. I use reusable plastic-coated wire for these joints; it grips the canes well. Guide young shoots onto their poles, using loose stem ties until they take hold.

A wigwam for a small space

If you haven't room for a long row, a wigwam makes good use of a small patch. Push the canes firmly into the soil in a circle, draw their tops together and tie the bundle securely. Plant at the bases and guide the shoots upwards. Leave room to reach round it for picking; a wigwam covered in beans is wider than its bare poles suggest.

Give peas plenty to catch

Peas need plenty of small things to catch hold of. I make a rectangular cane frame and fasten open climbing net to it. The net supports the plants; it is not insect-proof mesh. Twiggy pea sticks are a simpler option, or can help young plants reach the frame. Match the support height to the variety. Even dwarf peas benefit from short sticks. For an exposed spot, add diagonal bracing to the frame.

Broad beans need less

Tall broad beans in a windy spot can be supported by stout stakes and strings around the rows. Use extra stakes on long rows. Dwarf varieties usually need little or no support.

Sources: Hackriculture — Climbing Beans and Peas. RHS; National Botanic Garden of Wales.

Growing Techniques - Plant care

SupportsStakes, cages and wind

Some plants need a steadying hand, some want something to climb. Support the stem without gripping it tightly, and make sure the structure itself is firmly anchored.

A stake for tall brassicas

On an exposed plot, tall vegetables like Brussels sprouts can rock in the wind and take damage to leaves and roots. Drive a stout stake beside the plant, taking care around the roots. Use soft ties in a loose figure of eight between stem and stake, so they do not rub together. Check the ties as the stem thickens. In a sheltered garden, firm soil and earthing up may be enough.

Outdoor tomatoes in cages

Tomatoes can be staked too, but the weight of the fruit can break stems, particularly outdoors in wind. Consider making cages from stiff wire fencing. Cut a length, bend it into a cylinder and fasten the meeting edges with short wire ties. Turn sharp cut ends away from hands and plants, and peg the cage firmly down. Leave openings large enough for tending and picking. Guide stems through or tie them to the cage as needed; cordon tomatoes still need their usual training.

Brace a frame against the wind

A diagonal stops a frame folding sideways. Tie it firmly to the poles it crosses, with its lower end well secured. Use diagonals running in opposite directions along a bean row. A rectangular pea frame also needs bracing across its ends, as shown here. Check that joints cannot slide and the feet stay firm, then recheck after rough weather.

A temporary windbreak

Some cages and frames can do a second job in cold spring winds. Clip horticultural fleece around the outside as a low windbreak, keeping it clear of the plants and leaving the top open. Clothes pegs make a good reusable clip. Secure the fleece against flapping, check the anchoring, and remove it when the weather improves. This is shelter from wind, not a reason to plant tender crops before frost risk has passed.

Sources: Hackriculture — Climbing Beans and Peas; Outdoor Tomatoes. RHS — Staking.
