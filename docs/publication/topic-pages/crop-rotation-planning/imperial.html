Crop rotation & planning

Rotation means moving plant families to different ground instead of growing the same or similar crops in one place year after year. It has two useful jobs: reducing shared pests and diseases, and helping leafy crops use nitrogen left by peas and beans.

Keep a simple record

Sketch your beds and write down what grew where, including a second crop in the same year. Next season, move the main crop to ground that held an unrelated family. Changing cabbage for broccoli does not count: they share too many problems.

Let plants help each other

Bacteria in pea and bean root nodules fix nitrogen from the air. Leave healthy roots to decay for following leafy greens; add mature compost where needed. Manure or compost suits heavy eaters like potatoes and cucumbers; follow these with light feeders such as carrots.

BrassicasCabbage, kale, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cauliflower, kohlrabi, radish, swede and turnip

A rotation is not a disease cure

Clubroot and onion white rot, to take the two worst examples, can remain in soil for many years. A short circuit round the beds does not make infected ground clean. Keep contaminated soil off clean beds, and do not put infected allium debris into good compost.

Four beds, four years

The acme of rotation is the traditional four-block rotation system, as seen in many an old gardening manual. It’s prescriptive, but it gives you a clear plan for the whole plot. Divide the growing area into four beds or groups of beds, then move each crop group on one place each year.

Put each crop in its group

Crop rotation groups are not always obvious. In the four-block system, radishes, swedes and turnips belong with the brassicas. Onions and other roots share a block even though they include different families.

Plan the changeover

Keep recording every crop, including a second crop in the same year. A bed may still be occupied when the new year starts. Make the changeover after harvesting, in time for the next crop’s sowing or planting window.

Alternative strategy: A right British muddle

If you only have a small plot, or you have many different-sized beds, you can just mix plants up in small groups and let randomness and Nature do the rotation for you. It will still work; just keep things moving, and every year, do something different in each bed.
