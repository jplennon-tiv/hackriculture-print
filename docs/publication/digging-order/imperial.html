Growing Techniques - Cultivation

Dig or No-Dig Digging part 1

Single digging turns the topsoil. Double digging also loosens the layer underneath: a bigger job for a new or compacted bed, done at the start or occasionally, rather than every year.

Double digging: work in trenches

You need: A strong fork, digging spade, rake, barrow and well-rotted compost or manure.

1Open the first trench

Clear weeds and mark out the bed. Spread half your compost over it; keep the rest beside you. Dig across one end, one spade wide and deep. Save this topsoil in a barrow or at the far end for the final trench.

2Loosen underneath

Add a couple of spadefuls of compost to the trench bottom. Fork the lower soil another full fork-depth, mixing it in and removing rubble. Take small bites; use a mattock on hard patches. Loosen the subsoil in place, keeping it beneath the topsoil.

3Move along the bed

Dig the next trench alongside, turning its topsoil into the first and mixing in the surface compost. Break clods and remove weed roots. Now add compost and fork the bottom of this new trench, just as before. Repeat along the bed.

4Fill the final gap

Loosen the bottom of the last trench with compost, then fill it with the topsoil you saved at the start. Fork any remaining compost into the surface. Rake the top level and slope the sides; timber edging is optional.

5Settle and plant

Let the loosened soil settle. For small seeds, rake the surface into fine crumbs. Follow the crop’s guide for planting time, depth and spacing, then water in. If the bed must wait, cover it with a tarp; remove the cover and any slugs before planting.

Work when soil crumbles. If it is wet and sticky or frozen, wait. Take small bites and regular breaks.

Dig or No-Dig Digging part 2

Single digging: the annual job

On an established dug bed, spread well-rotted compost or manure where needed. Start at one edge and turn the ground over with a spade or fork, working backwards so you don’t tread on what you have loosened. Work to one spade’s depth, bury the compost, break large clods and remove stones and weed roots. There is no need to organise this into trenches.

Keep your feet on the paths

Permanent paths help prevent the bed being compacted again. Single digging is the usual yearly routine in a dug garden; reserve double digging for when the deeper soil needs loosening. You can switch to surface compost and no-dig as the bed improves.

Another option: Smothering

Dig or no-dig, your life becomes easier on overgrown ground if you kill the overgrowth first. Smothering does this by excluding light: cover the patch with an opaque tarpaulin, let the growth die back, then remove the cover and clear the remains.

1Cut back and cover

Cut tall growth and rake away the cuttings. Lever out bramble crowns and woody roots. Lay an opaque tarpaulin close to the ground, covering the whole patch. Weight its edges with bricks or stones so wind cannot lift it.

2Give it time

Allow around eight weeks in late spring or summer, or five months or more from late autumn or winter. Check underneath: if growth is still alive, leave it covered longer. Persistent weeds can take more time.

3Lift and clear

Remove the tarp. Rake or fork out dead growth, shake off the soil and compost the debris. Lift surviving perennial weeds with their roots intact; don’t chop them up. Then dig the bed or add compost for no-dig.
