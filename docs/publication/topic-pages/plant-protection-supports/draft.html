Growing Techniques - Plant care

NettingKeep pests outside

A well-fitted cover can save a crop before the trouble starts. Choose it for the pest you want to exclude, and make it easy to open: you will still need to look after the plants underneath.

Choose the covering for the job

Open netting

Check what the openings will exclude. A net that stops pigeons may let butterflies through; butterfly netting will not stop small flies.

Insect-proof mesh

Useful over brassicas, carrots and leeks. Match the grade to the pest: small insects need finer mesh. Check the supplier's description.

Horticultural fleece

Use fleece when young plants need extra warmth or protection from cold winds. It can share the same supports, but watch for overheating.

Give the plants room to grow

I use canes topped with empty bottles. The smooth glass saves the covering from snagging, and the height is easy to change. Push the canes firmly into the ground around the crop, put an intact bottle over each top, then lay the cover over them. Keep it clear of the leaves and hold every edge down with bricks or boards, closing the gaps. Leave enough material to raise it later; reseat any loosened canes firmly. Hoops or a reusable frame work too.

Look underneath

Check leaves for eggs and caterpillars, repair holes, and close the edges after weeding or picking. Recheck after rough weather. Watch moisture and heat under fine mesh. Rotate susceptible crops: covering ground that still contains their pests can shut the problem in with the plants.

Remember the pollinators

Open or remove covers when crops such as courgettes and squash flower and need insect visitors. Follow the crop guide: this is not a rule to uncover every flowering vegetable.

I prefer fine mesh after finding birds tangled in ordinary netting. Even so, secure it properly, avoid hanging loops and check for trapped wildlife.

SupportsHelp plants stand tall

Put supports in while plants are small, allowing for the weight of the crop and the wind catching its leaves. A frame that looks sturdy when empty has a much harder job later.

A firm frame for beans

For climbing beans, lean two rows of canes towards one another and join them with a pole along the ridge. I fasten the joints with reusable plastic-coated wire, which grips the canes well. On an exposed plot, add diagonal braces running in opposite directions along the rows. Smaller intermediate canes help shoots bridge wide gaps. A wigwam suits a smaller planting. Guide young shoots onto their supports, using loose stem ties until they take hold.

Give peas plenty to catch

Peas need plenty of small things to catch hold of. I make a rectangular cane frame, brace its sides and ends, and fasten climbing net to it. This open net supports the plants; it is not insect-proof mesh. Twiggy pea sticks are a simpler option, or can help young plants reach the frame. Match the height to the variety. Even dwarf peas benefit from short sticks.

Hold up heavy stems

Tie cordon tomatoes loosely to a sturdy cane or train them up a string. Outdoors, I also use cages made from stiff wire mesh joined into cylinders. Peg them down and brace them; a central cane gives extra support where needed. Cages do not replace the cordon's usual training. For tall broad beans in a windy spot, stout stakes with strings around the rows may be all you need.

Keep the useful bits

Check joints and stem ties as plants grow. At the end of the season, collect sound canes and reusable fastenings. A pot of last year's ties saves a surprising amount of fiddling.
