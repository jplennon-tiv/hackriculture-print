## Size and materials

Use a known object in the same depth plane to judge scale. John's climbing-bean canes are over 7 ft long, and the finished frame stands higher than he does. In [B, clip 0022 at 02:53](../../../publication/supports-photo-trial/bean-row-camera-review/B-0022-02-53.jpg), compare a single shaft with his hand and body. In [D, clip 0026 at 00:01](../../../publication/supports-photo-trial/bean-row-camera-review/D-0026-00-01.jpg), look at the assembled frame against the shed. Keep long shafts slender from a distance. A distant tip may almost disappear into the sky or a hedge. Choose the height and gauge afresh for shorter peas and freestanding broad beans.

Use the slightly weathered buff, grey and ochre of real garden bamboo, with small nodes, a taper and modest differences in diameter and length. The thicker butt goes into the soil. A cut tip is a narrow hollow tube. Its width continues naturally through the binding. A cane can be straight or bow gently in one direction under load. Keep a straight rail straight when that is what the photo shows. Vary tip heights and inclinations by a little. Exaggerated S-bends, repeated curves and poles of uniform thickness all misrepresent the material. A coated metal pole visible among the main canes is a separate component.

At a bamboo joint, look at [F, clip 0026 at 00:34](../../../publication/supports-photo-trial/bean-row-camera-review/F-0026-00-34.jpg). John uses short pieces of green plastic-coated wire. Each hugs the crossing, with a hand twist and short ends. Vary the winding, direction of the twist and visible tail from joint to joint. Keep them small. Pea netting has open squares for tendrils. Broad-bean support string runs along the outside of the plants. Both are distinct from the wire that binds a cane joint.

## Set out the feet before drawing the tops

Mark the near and far edges of the cultivated bed on the camera view. Place every foot in the soil, clear of the path. For two parallel rows, use [E, clip 0026 at 00:22](../../../publication/supports-photo-trial/bean-row-camera-review/E-0026-00-22.jpg) to see the space between their feet. On level ground the far line usually sits higher in the photograph. Mark the crossings and account for the depth of each. Follow each individual shaft all the way from its own foot through its joint to its tip. The middle pairs in a view across a row can appear nearly edge-on, and the projected lean can change across the picture. Preserve those overlaps. Two apparent legs ending at one foot, a shaft changing partners halfway up, or an extra end emerging from a crossing calls for a correction.

For a circular wigwam, project the circle of feet as an ellipse, with near and far feet on their respective arcs. For a rectangular frame, establish the four ground corners and the four corresponding upper corners. Put each rail, net, string and brace on its actual face. If the projection is hard to read, sketch the corners and continuous shafts on a working copy first. Small contact shadows and consistent cast shadows can help locate a foot. Keep a shadow distinguishable from the cane that casts it.

In [bottles over canes](../../../publication/netting-photo-trial/bottles-over-canes-01/Bottles-over-Canes.png), the bottle mouth fits over the upright and the closed base presents a smooth top to the fleece. Showing one support close up beside a few working together made the method easier to read.

For a finished structure, a person can be omitted. If John demonstrates a stage, compare his full-body proportions and clothing with the camera still and place his hands where the work calls for them. When removing him from an approved structure, protect the shafts, ties and ground contacts, including those crossing his shadow. An [empty take from the same camera](../../../publication/supports-photo-trial/bean-row-04/Empty-bed-0022-00m15s.jpg) shows what belongs behind him.

Inspect the full-resolution picture and then its actual page slot. Follow the front and rear shafts, identify every teaching joint, and check that plants differ in height, angle and spacing. If the wire or mesh disappears in print, adjust the viewpoint or background contrast, or use a separate close view where the page provides one. Keep believable cane diameters and the real bed, paths and landmarks.
