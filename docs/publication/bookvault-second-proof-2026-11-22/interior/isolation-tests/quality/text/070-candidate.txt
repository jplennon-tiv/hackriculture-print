Parsnips are slow, deep-rooting biennials grown for white tap roots that sweeten as cold turns starch to sugar.
They occupy the ground for a long season and seedlings emerge slowly, but established plants need little
beyond weed control and moisture while young. Use fresh seed: cold or dry soil and sowing too deeply can
leave a blank row. Deep, humus-rich, stone-free ground gives the best roots, preferably undug or improved for
an earlier crop; avoid fresh manure.

Recommended varieties
Gladiator
A well-established hybrid parsnip
producing useful, even roots. A
strong first choice for the main
autumn and winter crop.
Tender and True
A traditional parsnip with long
roots and good flavour. Best grown
in deep, stone-free soil.
Palace F1
An early-to-maincrop hybrid with
smooth roots, shallow crowns and
some canker resistance. A useful
alternative to Gladiator.
White Gem
Sweet, stocky roots for shallower
or heavier soil, with some canker
resistance.
Hollow Crown Improved
Long, tapering roots for cooking or
showing. Give them deep, loose
soil without stones.
The Student
Medium-sized, thick, tapering roots
with a sweet flavour.
Key risks
Canker
In wet winters, heavy soils,
acid soil or ground
containing fresh manure, the
tops of roots may develop
brown or black fungal rot.
Poor germination
Parsnip seed loses viability
quickly.
Carrot root fly
Carrot fly larvae tunnel into
parsnip roots, leaving rusty￾brown scars and making
damaged roots vulnerable to
rot.
Forking
Forked roots are usually
caused by stones, heavy soil,
fresh manure, lumpy
compost or ground that has
not been improved deeply
enough in advance.
