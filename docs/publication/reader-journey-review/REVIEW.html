The book works well as a practical crop reference. Most decisions can be made from the existing two-page spreads and Troubles pages. The final pass should improve the joins: interpreting dates, recognising the stage of a job, moving between crops, and finding advice by task.

Make seven focused editorial improvements, settle three specific copy decisions, and leave the book’s structure, page count and two-page crop format intact. The optional ideas can wait.

What is already working

The second page earns its place

The practical pages usually resolve the uncertainty left by a calendar or quick fact: seed versus sets, direct versus indoor sowing, heated versus unheated cover, and observable harvest cues.

Current book: p45, p67, p73, p75, p83

Troubles helps the reader take action

Crop-specific links lead to recognition, action and prevention. The allium pages distinguish leek moth from leaf miner; the tomato pages distinguish moisture disorders from pests. These routes should be preserved.

Current book: p65, p128, p140

Winter growing is already substantially covered

Kale, leeks, parsnips, sprouting broccoli and storage crops support an experienced reader’s full year. Useful succession advice already exists in the kale, garlic, turnip and Oriental-leaf guides.

Current book: p21, p31, p33, p41, p59, p62, p65, p79

The techniques are practical and conditional

Starting small, checking soil and weather, hardening plants, watering according to need and adapting rotation to small areas all work. A new beginner chapter or fixed monthly prescription is not needed.

A1 · First passCurrent book: p9

Make the calendar key describe what the colours actually mean

The problem. Muted months are described as options for warmer climates. The source also uses less-usual dates for protected starts, particular varieties and overwintering methods. Bright sowing months can include raising indoors. A reader can mistake a sowing window for an outdoor planting instruction.

Proposed edit. Revise the existing explanation and the “warmer climates” sublabel. Describe less-usual dates as conditional options, direct readers to the crop’s Sowing & planting instructions, and distinguish sowing from transplanting. Retain both colours and all source dates.

Why this earns its space

Evidence. Carrot has a protected late route; lettuce has protected early and overwintering routes; greenhouse cucumber distinguishes heated and unheated starts on its practical page. All three reader journeys encountered this mismatch.

Page space. Replace current words within the approved peach key panel. No new calendar, graphic or page.

Proposed edit. On p161, distinguish an earlier protected sowing/raising window from the later outdoor planting conditions. On p16 use “Planting depth”; on p88 use “Seed depth”. Preserve the existing methods, measurements and condition checks.

Why this earns its space

Evidence. P157 already says to delay in cold or wet weather. Outdoor cucumber and tomato have their own frost/warmth conditions. Canonical celeriac has no numeric sowing depth; its printed depth comes from planting_depth. Asparagus has separate seed and crown instructions.

Page space. One sentence replacement and two short label changes. No extra calendar row or altered planting method.

How the book carried each reader through the year

Each task follows a concrete lookup and decision. Outcomes describe what the book enables; they do not invent successful crops, measured yields or actual reader behaviour.

Five crops, two beds, no greenhouse

A first-time grower chooses carrot, loose-leaf lettuce, dwarf French beans, onion sets and one courgette. A modest harvest and useful experience are the aim. Winter can be a pause.

Works after reading both pages. The most significant obstacles are the calendar explanation and carrot-compost ambiguity.

JanuaryWhat is a sensible first selection, and can it fit in my two beds and available time?

Reader’s decision. Choose the five crops listed in the persona; choose dwarf beans, loose-leaf lettuce and onion sets. Reserve generous space for one courgette and plan short rows rather than planting every seed in a packet.

How the book helps. Answered with several page visits. The book positively supports starting small and choosing useful crops. Courgette spacing and frequent harvesting prevent the tempting but unsuitable decision to plant several in one small bed.

Friction or limitation. Difficulty measures growing ease, not time commitment. The courgette's daily/every-other-day picking obligation is clear on p73 but not visible in its difficulty score. This is a selection-stage discovery issue, not absent advice. A beginner route would reduce page hunting, but the contents already expose the relevant planning pages.

FebruaryHow do I prepare the existing beds, and should the carrot area receive the same compost as everything else?

Reader’s decision. Prepare access and remove weeds when the soil is workable. Plan the bed areas. Continue preparation for the other crops, but hold the decision to add new compost to the carrot area because the printed guidance appears inconsistent.

How the book helps. Most of the preparation route works; the carrot compost sub-decision stalls. The no-dig page gives a real sequence, materials, maturity requirements and ongoing maintenance, rather than merely advocating a method.

Friction or limitation. P14 says compost added shortly before sowing usually causes fanging, while its introduction says compost-filled no-dig beds can work; p151 permits planting into finished mature compost and p112 names fresh manure rather than compost in general. The discrepancy also exists across canonical records. See NOV-02.

MarchCan I start my onions without growing seedlings indoors, and can I start a few lettuces?

Reader’s decision. When conditions suit, plant Sturon sets root-end down with the tip showing, 4–6 in. apart in 12 in. rows. Start a small direct-sown lettuce batch, using its outdoor depth and eventually the loose-leaf spacing. If conditions do not suit, delay instead of following the month mechanically.

AprilHow do I sow carrots, and why does the harvest panel say August when the text mentions June?

Reader’s decision. Sow a short row directly when the soil is workable and warm enough, keep the germinating seed moist and record the date. Treat baby roots and an early harvest as different from the main August–October harvest window rather than waiting automatically until August.

How the book helps. The physical sowing and thinning job is answerable after reading both crop pages. The 17-day germination fact reduces the chance of declaring failure after a few days.

Friction or limitation. Early harvesting is present, but the page-wide calendar key describes muted June/July harvest options as warmer-climate options. Canonical notes also describe early short-rooted crops and protected starts. A reader has to reconcile scope rather than just read a colour. Drills and thinning are illustrated but not plainly defined; see NOV-01 and NOV-03.

JuneSomething has eaten the young lettuce. What should I check tonight, and can I replace lost plants?

Reader’s decision. Compare the actual damage before naming a pest, inspect after dark or in damp weather for slugs and remove those found. Keep damp debris away from vulnerable plants. Replace losses with a small sowing still within the stated lettuce window; modules are an available alternative, not a greenhouse requirement.

How the book helps. Answered with a short crop-to-trouble route. The diagnostic separation and an action that can be taken the same evening work well.

Friction or limitation. Using the general Slugs and Snails index entry produces many crop page references; following the crop footer is more efficient. This is not a missing slug-control section. 'Protect seedlings' is broad, but the same route gives hand-removal and debris management, so the reader is not left with that phrase alone.

JulyHow do I pick the three repeat crops without ending their harvest, and can my short daily visits keep up?

Reader’s decision. Pick larger outer lettuce leaves while retaining the centre and at least four young leaves; pick bean pods every few days before seeds swell; cut young courgettes, often 4–6 in., daily or every other day in warmth. Keep the one-plant limit and record whether that frequency is manageable.

How the book helps. Answered. The descriptions connect an action to keeping the plant productive. Courgette pollination guidance also distinguishes the female flower by the small fruit behind it, and the Troubles page warns that shrivelling can have more than one cause.

Friction or limitation. Success depends on visits between weekends. That limit becomes visible in the crop text and should affect next year's choice; it does not support calling the book's Easy/Fairly Easy ratings wrong. No additional harvesting chapter is needed for these crops.

Current book: p45, p57, p73, p117
AugustLeaves droop in hot weather. Should I water everything more, including onions whose tops are falling?

Reader’s decision. Check moisture before watering, apply water at the relevant rooting area and distinguish the still-growing crops from ripening onions. Lift onions when the described foliage condition is reached; dry on airy trays under cover in wet weather until necks and skins are dry.

How the book helps. Answered. The watering page is a strong diagnostic and workload guide, and prevents interpreting all drooping leaves as a demand for another can. The onion harvest cue is observable and more useful than a calendar date alone.

Friction or limitation. Mixed crops in one bed mean different watering stages. The book gives the necessary crop distinctions and root-directed method, but the reader must combine them. A fixed universal watering amount would be less helpful than the existing conditional advice.

SeptemberHow should I finish the onions and beans, and do I need to fill every gap with a winter crop?

Reader’s decision. Store sound, well-dried onions; use sound damaged or thick-necked bulbs first and discard rot. Keep picking useful beans while tender. Choose a winter pause after the chosen crops, rather than assuming a new late bean sowing or unspecified winter lettuce will work.

How the book helps. Answered for the limited first-year goal. The book permits a small useful garden instead of implying that empty winter space is failure. It offers optional overwintering routes without making them compulsory.

Friction or limitation. A reader seeking an uninterrupted winter salad crop would need a more specific variety/protection timetable than the generic lettuce text. That is outside this persona's modest first-year target and is not used to demand a new winter-growing chapter.

OctoberWhich carrots can be stored, and what do I do when the courgette is finished after frost?

Reader’s decision. Separate sound storage carrots from damaged or rotten roots, trim as specified, keep cool and ventilated and inspect them. For normal courgette clearance, combine the general compost principle with the crop condition, but flag that the crop-specific frost/healthy-remains instruction is not available on its own spread.

How the book helps. Carrot storage is answered; normal courgette closure is only indirectly answered. The current canonical courgette record supplies the omitted close-down instruction, so there is no research need to invent one.

Friction or limitation. This is a narrow missing crop instruction, not absence of all end-of-season advice: runner beans p47 explicitly mentions removing supports and composting stems after frost, and other crops also describe clearing. A novice following the courgette route has no reason to consult those unrelated crop pages. See NOV-05.

Reader’s decision. Record varieties, actual sowing and picking dates, pest episodes and which repeated picking jobs fitted the household. Inspect stored crops, removing rot as instructed. Use that evidence to keep the successful choices and reconsider the most demanding one next spring.

An established plot and a year-round ambition

An experienced grower has about 60 m², limited weekly time and crops carried over from the previous year. The test includes crop handovers, summer problems, winter harvests and stores.

The strongest journey. Winter and storage advice already works; a missing leek timing cue and treatment qualification are worth restoring.

JanuaryWhat can I keep picking now, and which beds can I release without sacrificing the spring harvest?

Reader’s decision. Pick outer kale leaves while preserving the growing centre; keep plants for late-winter/spring shoots. Lift leeks and parsnips as needed, lifting a batch before frozen soil prevents access. Keep sprouting broccoli in place for late winter/spring rather than clearing it simply because summer cropping has ended. Sketch beds and record both the main and second crop family.

How the book helps. Successful. Kale explains continued spring use, parsnips explain in-ground holding and the end point before regrowth, and leeks explain heeling in to free a bed. Broccoli names overwintering types and gives approximately 44 weeks from sowing, so its next-year harvest is not an unsupported assumption.

Friction or limitation. There is no winter-harvest or space-reuse index entry; the grower must start from known crop names. This is a navigation opportunity, not missing winter guidance.

Reader’s decision. Choose a first-early potato for the later leek handover, rather than a maincrop that occupies the bed longer. Keep seed potatoes bright, cool and frost-free if sprouting them. Raise only a small protected lettuce batch; wait for sensible soil/weather before outdoor work.
