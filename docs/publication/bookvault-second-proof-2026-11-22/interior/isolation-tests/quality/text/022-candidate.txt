Leaf beets suit gardeners who enjoy spinach but find it quick to bolt or difficult in dry weather. Swiss chard,
seakale beet, spinach beet and perpetual spinach are grown for leaves and stems; a few plants give repeated
harvests over a long season. Chard has broad leaves and thick, edible midribs in white, red, yellow or pink,
making it attractive in a flower border. Spinach beet has plainer green leaves, used like spinach. Pick regularly:
young leaves are tender for salads or cooking, while oversized leaves toughen and growth slows. In mild
winters, established plants may give an early-spring flush before flowering.

Recommended varieties
Bright Lights
A colourful chard selection with
stems in several shades. Harvest
young leaves for salads or larger
leaves and stems for cooking.
Rhubarb Chard
Red stalks and dark leaves make
this chard decorative as well as
useful. Treat it as a red-stemmed
type unless a cultivar is specified.
Spinach Beet
A leaf beet grown for repeated
picking of green leaves. Useful as a
cooking green through a longer
season than true spinach.
Fordhook Giant
A white-stemmed chard with
broad green leaves. A useful
kitchen choice when leaf yield
matters more than mixed stem
colours.
Lucullus
Pale stems and crinkled green
leaves. Pick the outer leaves
regularly for a continuing crop.
White Silver 2
Broad green leaves and white,
fleshy stalks. Useful for both leafy
greens and cooked stems.
Key risks
Slugs
Leaf beet is usually hardy and trouble￾free, but slugs may nibble young
plants, especially in autumn or damp
weather.
Manganese deficiency
Yellowing between leaf veins can
indicate a nutrient problem, but
symptoms overlap.
Leaf spot
Leaf spots may have pale brown
centres and dark brown or purple
margins, roughly 1/4 in. across.
